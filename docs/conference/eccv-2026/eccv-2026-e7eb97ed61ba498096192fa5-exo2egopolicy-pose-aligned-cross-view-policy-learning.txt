Abstract. Learning robot manipulation policies from large-scale hu-
man videos is challenging due to the viewpoint mismatch between third-
person observations and egocentric robot control. To this end, we intro-
duce Exo2EgoPolicy, a geometry-aware framework that learns egocentric
manipulation policies from monocular exocentric video via viewpoint-
aligned pose translation. Unlike prior Exo→Ego approaches that rely on
diffusion-based pixel synthesis and often produce temporally inconsistent
predictions, our method operates directly on a structured pose mani-
fold and explicitly models the geometric ambiguity inherent in monoc-
ular observation. We formulate exocentric-to-egocentric translation as a
viewpoint-conditioned latent variable model on the SE(3) manifold that
disentangles pose from camera transformation and captures the ambi-
guity induced by unknown camera extrinsics. Under bounded viewpoint
motion, the formulation yields pose trajectories that are identifiable up
to a global rigid transformation and supports stable sequential infer-
ence through temporal regularization. Beyond representation alignment,
we show that for quasi-static manipulation tasks whose rewards de-
pend primarily on relative hand–object geometry, pose captures the key
task-relevant information required for policy learning. This enables pol-
icy co-training on pose-translated human demonstrations alongside lim-
ited robot teleoperation data within a unified human–robot state space.
Empirically, Exo2EgoPolicy improves temporal consistency, cross-view
alignment, and out-of-distribution policy transfer compared to pixel-
based translation and other cross-view co-training baselines, achieving

Introduction
From early developmental stages, humans observe external actions and seam-
lessly internalize them into their own motor repertoire. Driven by mirror-neuron
mechanisms, we can effortlessly infer the spatial relationships and hand–object
interactions that would be perceived from a first-person perspective simply by
watching someone else act. This actor–observer translation ability underpins
human imitation learning [22] and has profound implications for robotics [15],
augmented/virtual reality [23], and embodied intelligence [55]. In robot learning,
projecting a human instructor’s hand–object interactions into the robot’s own
viewpoint enables direct policy alignment with demonstrations [19,80].
Despite the well-established utility of egocentric (first person) data for ma-
nipulation learning [20,79], the overwhelming majority of human demonstrations
are recorded in exocentric (third-person) formats. Bridging this viewpoint gap
is fundamentally challenging. Unlike standard novel view synthesis, exocentric-
to-egocentric (Exo-to-Ego) translation involves extreme viewpoint shifts where
critical regions are fully occluded [28]. For example, the inner pages of a book or
the fine-grained contact geometry between fingers and objects may be invisible in
the exocentric recording but must be inferred in the egocentric perspective. The
problem is therefore inherently underdetermined. Existing approaches attempt
to resolve this challenge through either geometry-aware regression or generative
modeling. Geometry-based methods such as NeRF-style approaches [65,72] are
fundamentally regressive: they require dense multi-view supervision and struggle
under single-view input with heavy occlusion. Conversely, generative diffusion
models [11,34,44] can hallucinate unseen regions, but they operate in pixel space
and often exhibit geometric drift and temporal instability. While such generative
models may achieve strong perceptual metrics (e.g., FID or LPIPS), they lack the
structural guarantees required for downstream robotic control. Frame-wise pixel
hallucination further introduces high-frequency jitter and spatial inconsistencies
that corrupt continuous kinematic trajectories, rendering them unsuitable for
derivative-based manipulation policies.
We argue that this reflects a deeper misalignment in problem formulation.
Prior works treat Exo-to-Ego translation purely as a visual synthesis task. How-
ever, for robotic manipulation, the objective is not photorealistic rendering, but
recovery of action-relevant geometric structure. Manipulation rewards depend
primarily on relative hand–object pose, rather than on background texture fi-
delity. Pixel-level hallucination, therefore, introduces unnecessary variance while
failing to enforce temporal and geometric consistency.
To address this, we propose a paradigm shift from pixel synthesis to struc-
tured geometric alignment. We introduce Exo2EgoPolicy (shown in Figure 1),

Related Works
2.1
Exo-to-Ego View Generation
Exo-to-ego view generation addresses the substantial geometric and appearance
gap between third- and first-person perspectives. Early approaches [34,44] con-
dition egocentric synthesis via channel-wise concatenation of exocentric features.
However, the lack of pixel-level correspondence across viewpoints introduces sig-
nificant spatial misalignment, limiting geometric reasoning and often degrading
synthesis fidelity. 4Diff [11] improves cross-view fusion through cross-attention,
yet departs from fully pretrained diffusion backbones, which may limit general-
ization and scalability. Subsequent methods reduce ambiguity through additional
supervision or multi-view constraints. EgoExo-Gen [64] conditions generation
on the first egocentric frame, effectively narrowing the viewpoint gap but lim-
iting applicability when egocentric initialization is unavailable. Exo2Ego-V [29]
performs full video translation using multiple exocentric inputs and decoupled
spatial–temporal modules, which constrains scalability and weakens unified spa-
tio–temporal modeling. More broadly, recent video diffusion models [9,18,57,71]
have enabled high-quality conditional synthesis, including depth-, image-, and
camera-controlled generation [35, 66, 73]. Camera-control approaches typically
condition directly on camera extrinsics or leverage intermediate 3D representa-
tions [3,4,37,49]. However, these techniques are primarily designed for moderate
viewpoint perturbations and struggle under the extreme pose shifts inherent in
exocentric-to-egocentric translation.
In contrast, we move beyond pixel-level synthesis and formulate Exo-to-Ego
generation as geometry-aware pose inference on the SE(3) manifold. By explic-
itly disentangling viewpoint from intrinsic hand–object geometry and enforcing
temporal stability, our framework directly produces control-ready pose trajecto-
ries rather than appearance-only video synthesis.
2.2
Imitation Learning and Learning from Human Videos
Robot imitation learning has demonstrated substantial improvements in dexter-
ity and robustness using large-scale robot-collected datasets [12,33,60,74,78], fa-
cilitated by advances in scalable data collection [1,13,69]. Despite these advances,
robotics still lacks internet-scale embodied datasets comparable to vision [47]
and language [41], limiting open-world generalization across tasks and environ-
ments. To leverage abundant human videos [8, 39, 51, 70], prior work typically
aligns either observations or actions. Observation-alignment methods rely on in-
termediate representations such as affordances [2], object keypoints [6,14,63], or
structured object embeddings [36, 38]. While effective, these modular pipelines
can introduce compounding perceptual errors and complicate end-to-end credit
assignment.

Action-alignment approaches address embodiment mismatch via trajectory

hardware-based hand tracking [59], or teleoperation-based pose remapping such
as HumanPlus [16]. In contrast, our method does not require hardware in-

2.3
Cross-Embodiment
Cross-embodiment pre-training improves adaptability and generalization across
heterogeneous robotic platforms [10,17,53,67,76]. Recent end-to-end approaches
using cross-robot data [31,56] reduce compounding perceptual errors and exhibit
emergent corrective behaviors such as automatic retrying [7]. Other methods
enforce viewpoint constraints between human demonstrations and deployment
environments [27], trading scalability for a reduced domain gap.
Concurrent work explores learning directly from egocentric human demon-
strations [58]. DexCap [59] employs glove-based 3D tracking with RGBD cap-
ture, while EgoMimic [25] relies on closely matched egocentric camera view-
points. In contrast, our approach scales across diverse camera configurations
without alignment constraints or specialized hardware. By coupling geometry-
aware Exo-to-Ego pose translation with policy co-training, our approach enables
scalable cross-embodiment learning from heterogeneous human data.
3
Proposed Method
We present Exo2EgoPolicy, a geometry-aware framework for transferring manip-
ulation skills from third-person (exocentric) human demonstrations to egocentric
robot control. Rather than synthesizing novel-view pixels, which are computa-
tionally expensive and may introduce task-irrelevant visual artifacts, our key in-
sight is to translate only the hand-object kinematic state. The framework consists
of four tightly coupled components. First, we formulate Exo-to-Ego translation as
a viewpoint-conditioned latent diffusion model over the SE(3) manifold, resolv-
ing the geometric ambiguity inherent to single-view projection (subsection 3.1).
Second, to prevent the frame-to-frame jitter that arises from independent per-
frame generation, we augment the model with a temporal regularization using a
motion prior which ensures bounded cumulative trajectory error (subsection 3.2).
Third, we provide a formal Markov Decision Process (MDP) argument showing
that this viewpoint-aligned pose is a sufficient statistic for optimal policy learn-
ing in manipulation tasks (subsection 3.3). Finally, we describe a co-training
scheme that combines large-scale translated human demonstrations with limited
robot teleoperation data in a unified state-action space (subsection 3.4). The
overall workflow is shown in Figure 2.

Experiments and Results
4.1
Dataset and Implementation Details
The encoder Ei is a pretrained DINOv2-ViT-L/14 [42] projecting to d=512. The
pose encoder Ep is a three-layer MLP with hidden dimension 256 and ReLU
activations. Hand and object poses are extracted via HAMER [45] and Founda-
tionPose [62], respectively, yielding zt ∈R563. The score network sψ is a 12-layer
Transformer (8 heads, hidden dim 512) taking as input the embedded noisy pose,
a sinusoidal diffusion-time encoding, the flattened context ct, and the previous
pose pt−1, with a linear head. We adopt a VP-SDE with cosine noise sched-
ule (N=1000 training steps, N=50 DDIM steps at inference) and weighting
λ(τ) = 1 −cos(πτ/2). The motion prior Φ is a two-layer GRU (hidden size 256)
pretrained for 20 epochs on ground-truth sequences, with spectral normaliza-
tion enforcing L=0.95. The full translation module is trained on training splits
of OakInk V2 [75], H2O [26], AssemblyHands [40], and Assembly101 [52] for
200 epochs, batch size 128, AdamW (lr=3×10−4, cosine annealing, 5K warmup,
weight decay 10−4) on 8 A100 GPUs, with temporal consistency weight β=0.5.
For policy co-training, we follow the dataset composition and training protocol
of [68], including the robot teleoperation data, task suite, action representa-
tion, and all associated hyperparameters. The only modification is the addition
of Dtrans
human, consisting of exo-to-ego translated clips from TACO [32] and Mim-
icplay [58] spanning the same task categories. These samples are incorporated
through the co-training objective with balance weight µ=0.3, selected by match-
ing the gradient norms from the two data sources at the start of training.
To provide dense geometric grounding, we augment our pose representation
by attaching a lightweight mesh reconstruction head that recovers 3D hand and
object meshes from the predicted hand joint parameters via the differentiable
MANO layer [50] and object-specific shape priors. For comparability with prior
work, we follow standard evaluation protocols [43,45] and report both 3D joint
and mesh accuracy: PA-MPJPE and AUCJ for joint accuracy, and PA-MPVPE,
AUCV , F@5mm, and F@15mm for mesh accuracy. Policy evaluation is conducted
on the Ego Humanoid Manipulation Benchmark [68], built in NVIDIA Isaac Lab
with a Unitree H1 humanoid robot, following the exact evaluation protocol, task
definitions, and success criteria defined therein. Additional details are in the
supplementary file.

Policy Co-Training Evaluation
Simulation setup. We evaluate policy performance under co-training using the
Ego Humanoid Manipulation Benchmark [68], built on NVIDIA Isaac Lab with a
Unitree H1 humanoid robot equipped with two Inspire dexterous robotic hands.
The benchmark comprises 12 manipulation tasks categorized as either Short-

Baselines. We compare against four categories of methods, each probing a dif-
ferent aspect of the proposed pipeline. Robot-only (ACT [77] and EgoVLA-
NoPretrain [68]) train solely on Drobot without any human data, establishing
the data-scarce baseline. Exo-only co-training injects exocentric poses directly
into co-training without any viewpoint translation, isolating whether the benefit
comes from more data alone or from the viewpoint translation step itself. Pixel-
space co-training methods (EgoWorld [44], 4Diff [11], EgoX [24]) first synthesize
egocentric videos from exocentric input, extract poses via HAMER [45] and
FoundationPose [62], and co-train the same policy, directly measuring whether
pose quality of the translated poses affect policy learning. EgoVLA [68] pretrains
on egocentric human videos, representing the strongest prior method that uses
human data but avoids the exo-to-ego problem entirely. Ours (Exo2EgoPolicy)
uses the full pipeline with all contributions.

4.4
Ablation Study
To isolate the contribution of each design choice in Exo2EgoPolicy, we ablate
four design decisions on both pose translation quality (PA-MPJPE↓, F@15mm↑)
and downstream policy performance (mean SR↑on short- and long-horizon tasks
under the Unseen configuration), as shown in Table 5.
Object-relative formulation: Removing the object-relative coordinate frame
(row a) and predicting poses in absolute world coordinates causes a substan-
tial drop in both PA-MPJPE (11.4→18.4) and short-horizon SR (73.7→58.3%).
Without the object as a locally stable reference, the model must implicitly dis-
entangle camera motion from hand-object kinematics, which is ill-posed under
uncalibrated exocentric cameras and leads to systematically misaligned poses.
Geometric latent encoding: Replacing the geometry-aware latent representa-
tion zt with direct regression (row b) produces the lowest pose accuracy overall
(PA-MPJPE: 21.6), confirming that the explicit geometric encoding is essential
for resolving viewpoint ambiguity rather than memorizing appearance-to-pose
mappings. Autoregressive conditioning: Removing pt−1 from the score net-
work (row c) eliminates temporal context entirely, degrading both pose quality
and policy performance. This confirms that conditioning on the previous state is

Conclusion
We present Exo2EgoPolicy, a framework that translates exocentric human demon-
strations into egocentric robot actions directly in pose space, avoiding arti-
facts introduced by pixel-space view synthesis. A viewpoint-conditioned diffusion
model on SE(3), coupled with a temporally regularized dynamics prior, produces
smooth, geometrically consistent pose trajectories that serve as a sufficient rep-
resentation for policy learning. Experiments on pose translation benchmarks and
the Ego Humanoid Manipulation Benchmark demonstrate that exocentric hu-
man video is a scalable and effective source of manipulation knowledge, with
consistent gains over all baselines especially on long-horizon tasks.
