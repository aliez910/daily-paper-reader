# Abstract 

Recent Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models map visual-textual inputs to robotic actions via end-to-end architec-tures, yet this approach entangles visual understanding with task-specific actions. This leads to an exhaustive collec-tion of full operational sequences and parameter redun-dancy across tasks, while generic third-person camera se-tups require fine-tuning for different hardware due to im-plicit hand-eye assumptions. We argue that decoupling how robots see from how robots act is a missing primitive in VLA systems. We present EgoRoC , a plug-and-play ego-centric alignment head that precedes any task policy and exposes only a thin 6-DoF pose interface. EgoRoC es-tablishes task-agnostic viewpoint consistency from a wrist-mounted (first-person) camera and then alternates align-ment with manipulation, while a diffusion-based online hand–eye module corrects the action in the end-effector frame for hardware-agnostic deployment. Trained once from static wrist–target image pairs with relative poses, rather than full manipulation trajectories, EgoRoC leaves downstream VLAs unchanged. By turning egocentric align-ment into a reusable capability, EgoRoC reduces training redundancy, strengthens zero-shot cross-scene transfer, and scales across VLA backbones without manual calibration. Across simulation and real settings, attaching EgoRoC con-sistently boosts success rates, especially on long-horizon and out-of-distribution tasks, and improves data efficiency during fine-tuning.

We introduce EgoRoC , a paradigm that decouples ego-centric visual alignment from task-specific manipulation. Motivated by how humans adjust viewpoints before acting (e.g., technicians aligning microscopes, surgeons position-ing arthroscopes), EgoRoC provides a plug-and-play vi-sual frontend that aligns a robot’s first-person view with a task-relevant target scene prior to manipulation. As il-lustrated in Fig. 1, EgoRoC establishes task-agnostic ego-centric alignment using a wrist-mounted camera (first-person observations). The head attaches to off-the-shelf VLAs through a thin 6-DoF pose interface, requiring no architecture-specific changes or joint retraining. Empiri-cally, existing VLAs already benefit from this division: for instance, OpenVLA’s “Put Eggplant into Pot” includes an “Easy Version” that manually initializes the end-effector above the target before policy execution. By focusing on viewpoint consistency, our approach enables cross-task skill transfer, reduces training to static image pairs rather than full trajectories, and improves downstream policy robust-ness as a drop-in component. To realize this plug-and-play ego workflow, the robot performs egocentric alignment upstream of task execution. Given the current first-person observation from the wrist camera, a task-oriented target image, and a textual prompt, the EgoRoC head outputs an adjustment pose consumable by standard VLAs or lightweight task operators via a simple API. Following established VLA practices [2, 11, 28, 31, 58], we fine-tune injected learnable adapters [19] in LMMs to strengthen visual alignment, while leaving downstream task policies intact. However, the hand–eye relationship between a wrist-mounted camera and the robotic arm can bias the adjust-ment pose. We therefore propose a lightweight online hand–eye calibration module based on Conditional Diffu-sion Models [45] to correct the visual alignment outputs. Unlike classical calibration pipelines, our module calibrates 

during active operation and generalizes to arbitrary cam-era–arm assemblies without CAD models or measurement tools. Our key insight is that deviations in the hand–eye system are implicitly encoded in the inputs, including im-age pairs (current and target) and the robot’s executed ac-tions. We thus develop a cross-modal attention mechanism that fuses image correspondence features with action pa-rameters to produce conditional features that guide the dif-fusion process. To robustly extract these implicit encodings across scenes where the hand–eye relation is fixed but vi-sual context varies, we further introduce a geometry-aware matching mechanism that derives spatial relationships via learnable, geometry-guided differential features. By elevating egocentric alignment to a plug-and-play ca-pability, rather than an incidental byproduct of task learn-ing, we enable robots to “ learn to see ” before they “ learn to do ”, while adhering to a minimal-change principle for existing systems. Extensive experiments show consistent gains in task success rates, strong zero-shot cross-scene transfer, and data-efficient fine-tuning, with drop-in com-patibility across widely used VLAs. Our contributions are threefold: • Plug-and-Play, Task-Agnostic Visual Alignment. Aprincipled formulation of egocentric visual alignment as a reusable, drop-in head for diverse manipulation tasks, reducing implicit redundancy in multi-task training and requiring no changes to downstream VLA policies. • Data-Efficient Training. A paradigm that leverages static image pairs with relative poses for training, remov-ing the need for full task-specific manipulation data and significantly lowering data collection requirements. • Online Hand–Eye Calibration. A diffusion-based on-line calibration module that adapts visual alignment outputs to real-time robot operation, ensuring cross-platform compatibility without manual tuning. 

# 2. Related Work 

In this paper, we primarily review Large Multimodal Mod-els (LMMs), VLA models, and online hand–eye calibration in relation to our work. 

2.1. Large Multimodal Models 

The emergence of Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) like LLaVa [31] and GPT-4 [1] has expanded perception and reasoning through strong task generalization and multi-step inference. Building on these advances, a series of LMMs [2, 7, 9–11, 28, 31, 32, 56, 58] exhibit robust ca-pability over images, videos, and text. In our setting, the LMM serves as an egocentric visual alignment front-end rather than a full manipulation policy. We adopt the open-source Qwen2.5-VL-7B [3] as the backbone, which performs strongly on MMMU [53]. We fine-tune only lightweight adapters to endow alignment ability while keep-ing downstream task policies unchanged. 

2.2. Vision-Language-Action Models 

Recently proposed Vision–Language–Action (VLA) mod-els, built on pre-trained LMMs [8, 14, 31, 35], have con-nected semantic reasoning with control for robotic ma-nipulation. Early works such as Palm-E [12] and RT-1 [6] established large-scale transformer training for real-world control, and RT-2 [5] further leveraged web-scale vision–language knowledge via PaLI-X [8]. 2.3. Online Hand-Eye Calibration 

Hand–eye calibration estimates the rigid transform between an end-effector and an attached camera. Classical meth-ods such as Tsai–Lenz [48] and Horaud [18] formulate 

AX = XB with point correspondences and rigid mo-tions, but often require controlled setups and struggle when camera poses shift relative to the robot base [18, 48, 57]. Learning-based approaches [26, 27, 36] improve flexibility yet typically rely on external third-person views and mea-surement tools. In contrast, we perform online calibration directly from first-person wrist views during operation, us-ing a conditional generative module to convert/correct ego-centric alignment into the end-effector frame. This design avoids CAD or external markers and supports hardware-agnostic deployment by fusing correspondence cues and ex-ecuted actions into a compact calibration descriptor. 

# 3. Methodology 

3.1. Overview 

As illustrated in Fig. 2, EgoRoC contains two sequential stages. The first is Egocentric Visual Alignment Module (EVAM, Sec. 3.2) . EVAM estimates a 6-DoF relative trans-form that aligns the current egocentric observation with a task-relevant target view. The second is Diffusion-based Hand–Eye Calibration Module (DHCM, Sec. 3.3) . This stage converts and corrects EVAM outputs into the end-effector frame by estimating pose discrepancies between the wrist-mounted camera and end-effector, as well as compen-sating for small EVAM deviations. 

Deployment Workflow. Given a natural-language prompt, EVAM parses the ordered list of objects to be ma-nipulated and their temporal dependencies. This sequence conditions EVAM to render an ordered set of top-view tar-get images using a third-person camera (details in 3.2). At runtime, the host VLA remains unchanged and operates in alternation with EgoRoC. For each subgoal, EVAM predicts the transform, DHCM refines this transform, and the robot controller applies the refined motion. Control transfers to the host VLA for manipulation upon an aligned status, pro-34993 ceeding to the next subgoal after completion. If the object leaves the field of view ( searching status), EVAM reinitial-izes alignment. This loop enables long-horizon execution without policy re-training. 

3.2. Egocentric Visual Alignment 

As shown in Fig. 2, we parse object entities and temporal dependencies from task prompts (e.g., “Put the carrot on plate.”) using an LMM front-end. In this paper, we em-ploy Qwen2.5-VL-7B for this parsing step. We then use VGGT [50] with a third-person camera to construct a point map and render a temporally ordered top-view sequence 

Itar carrot → Itar plate . To enable real-time deployment and in-paint rendered images, we fine-tune pix2pix-turbo [40] with supervision distilled from Gemini 2.5 Flash Image [15]. This target sequence is generated once at the beginning of inference. EVAM then iteratively estimates the relative pose between the current egocentric view and the next target view until alignment is achieved. 

Architecture Design. We use Qwen2.5-VL-7B [3] as the backbone to handle varying image resolutions. The vi-sion encoder encodes the current image Icur and a selected target image Itar . The language model (Qwen2.5 [52]) pro-cesses EVAM prompts T that specify the alignment goal. Following [5], we discretize continuous action parameters via percentile-based binning (ranging from 1% to 99%) into 256 tokens per action dimension. EVAM predicts a 6-DoF relative transformation action A ∈ SE(3) with A ⋍ ⟨R, t⟩,where R ∈ SO(3) and t ∈ R3. In addition, we employ a dedicated align state token to indicate three phases: align-ing, aligned, or searching (object temporarily out of view). 

Training Protocol. We fine-tune only injected LoRA adapters [19] in the vision encoder, the multimodal projec-tion layer, and selected transformer layers of the language model. The optimization objective is next-token cross-entropy over action tokens. All other backbone weights remain frozen. While our default EVAM backbone is Qwen2.5-VL-7B, EVAM can also share the host VLA’s LMM (see Sec. 4.4).

3.4. Multi-Stage Training Strategy 

All data used in the training process consist of wrist-mounted camera image pairs with relative poses, which are extracted from complete robot manipulation episodes. We split the training set into two subsets. 

First Training Stage. We train EVAM and DHCM sep-arately on one subset. EVAM learns to predict the rel-ative pose between views under direct supervision from episode annotations. For DHCM, we randomly sample sev-eral calibration matrices X per image pair and transform the ground-truth relative pose P to obtain P′ as the input to the Text-MLP branch. The sampled X serve as labels for con-trastive learning, diffusion denoising, and geometric con-straints. This stage yields a view-alignment estimator for EVAM to estimate the relative pose between views robustly and a calibration modeler for DHCM to solve hand-eye cal-ibration using vision-text features. 

Second Training Stage. We perform end-to-end refine-ment on the remaining subset. We freeze EVAM and the Geo-MLP branches in DHCM, and train the cross-modal fusion and the diffusion generator. The Text-MLP input is the 6-DoF relative transformation predicted by EVAM, while the supervision is the episode-derived relative pose. # 4. Experiments 

4.1. Implementation Details 

Data Collection. We train EgoRoC with 2.3M ego-centric–target image pairs extracted from 60 k manipu-lation episodes selected from BridgeData [13, 49] and DROID [22], both included in Open X-Embodiment [39]. Each episode can provide multiple pairs with known rela-tive poses. 

Training Settings. As described in Sec. 3.4, EgoRoC em-ploys a multi-stage training strategy. In the first stage, we use a subset containing 80% total data to train EVAM for 

17 epochs with AdamW [34] at a learning rate of 10 −5,employing LoRA [19] with a rank of 64 , without warm-up, and with cosine annealing. This stage employs Deep-Speed ZeRO-2 optimization [42] without quantization. For DHCM, we set diffusion steps to 10 , using Adam [25] with a constant lr is 10 −4, weight decay is 5 × 10 −5, batch size is 1024 , and train for 50 , 000 steps. In the second stage, we use a subset with 20% total data, training for 1 epoch with SGD optimizer [44] at a constant lr of 10 −5 and batch size of 256 .

Baseline Methods and Metrics. We utilize 5 SOTA VLA baselines: OpenVLA [23], TinyVLA [51], Spa-tialVLA [41], Pi-0 [4], and OpenVLA-OFT [24]. We use “OFT” to indicate OpenVLA-OFT. Following prior work [23, 51], we report manipulation success rate .

Evaluation Protocol. At test time, EgoRoC is used as a plug-and-play head with each baseline. As de-scribed in Sec. 3.2, EgoRoC initially decomposes the over-all task into an ordered sequence of operational units. The execution then alternates between EgoRoC alignment and VLA manipulation: ⟨EgoRoC: visual alignment →

VLA: manipulation ⟩ per subgoal, handoff to the VLA oc-curs when EgoRoC reports aligned . Actions are absolute end-effector poses in SE(3) (3D translation & rotation). 

Evaluation Task. We evaluate EgoRoC with baselines across two task categories. Short-Horizon tasks: • Lift < object > : assesses grasping precision. • Put < object 1 > onto < object 2 >: evaluates sequen-tial reasoning and persistent visual alignment. Long-Horizon tasks: • Put all < object > into < object > : evaluates precise manipulation across multiple rounds. • Put < object > into a drawer : assesses precise, long-duration manipulation under spatial constraints and re-quired action order. For clarity, we abbreviate the 4 manipulation tasks as fol-lows: Lift < object > (Task-L), Put < object 1 > onto <object 2 > (Task-Po), Put all < object > into < object > 

(Task-PA), and Put < object > into a drawer (Task-PD).

In this experiment, we evaluate all five baselines, each sub-jected to 100 validation trials per task across a diverse set of objects. 4.3. Data-Efficient Fine-Tuning 

4.3.1. Setup 

To verify EgoRoC’s contribution to data-efficient VLA fine-tuning, we evaluate two baselines (OpenVLA and Pi-0) with the following protocol for each: • First Stage: Using the real-world setup described in Sec. 4.2.1, we collect 500 demonstrations of tasks un-related to our evaluation tasks and fine-tune the baseline for 20 epochs to adapt it to our environment domain. • Second Stage: We collect 200 demonstrations each for 

Task-L and Task-PD , and randomly sample 100 demon-strations from the first-stage set. We construct four mixed datasets of total sizes {75 , 150 , 225 , 300 } by sam-pling from the first and second stages in a 1:2 ratio, with the guarantee that smaller sets are subsets of larger ones. • Third Stage: Starting from the pre-adapted model of the first stage, we fine-tune on each mixed dataset for 10 

epochs. • Fourth Stage: We evaluate the four resulting models on 

Task-L , Task-Po , Task-PA , and Task-PD and quantify how attaching EgoRoC changes the success rate of each baseline. 

4.3.2. Real-World Fine-Tuning Performance 

As shown in Fig. 5, adding EgoRoC consistently raises suc-cess rates for both OpenVLA and Pi-0 with the same num-ber of demonstrations. The improvement is visible across 

In Distribution tasks (Task-L and Task-PD) and Out of Dis-tribution tasks (Task-Po and Task-PA), where alignment re-duces the spatial search burden and allows the fine-tuned policies to focus on manipulation. The curves in the In Dis-tribution block show that the dashed lines stay above their solid counterparts throughout, and the gap grows with addi-tional data. A similar trend appears in the Out of Distribu-tion block, where the benefit of EgoRoC is larger, indicating stronger generalization after alignment-driven fine-tuning.

# 5. Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced EgoRoC, a plug-and-play ego-centric alignment head for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) systems. By decoupling visual alignment from task execu-tion, EgoRoC enables robots to “learn to see” before “learn-ing to do”, allowing downstream policies to focus exclu-sively on manipulation. The modular design ensures seam-less integration with existing VLAs, requiring no changes to their task-specific policies. Extensive experiments demon-strate that EgoRoC consistently improves task success rates, particularly in long-horizon and out-of-domain settings. It also enhances zero-shot cross-scene transfer and data-efficient fine-tuning, underscoring its practical value for real-world robotic systems. 

Limitations and Future Work. While EgoRoC achieves strong performance, its reliance on static image pairs may limit its ability to handle highly dynamic environments. Fu-ture work will explore temporal alignment techniques and more efficient online calibration to further reduce latency in real-time applications.
