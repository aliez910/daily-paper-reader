## 1. Introduction 

Robotic manipulation is moving beyond short, single-step primitives toward long-horizon, open-world tasks in which goals, layouts, and human preferences evolve over time. In these settings, an agent must not only see and act but also maintain an interpretable decision chain that remains reli-able under shifting spatial relations and temporal plans. However, achieving such reliability is hindered by two primary bottlenecks. (1) On the spatial side, language-to-action grounding is brittle: natural-language instructions are often ambiguous (e.g., “pour tea into the cup” in a multi-cup scene) or underspecified (e.g., “place the book left of the mug,” leaving the exact target pose undefined). To re-solve referential uncertainty and translate linguistic rela-tions into executable constraints, we advocate explicit vi-sual sketches (i.e., region highlights, keypoints, and rela-tional arrows anchored to scene geometry). (2) On the tem-poral side, human-in-the-loop coordination remains weak: real-time interaction is limited, and explainable planning 

artifacts are rarely exposed, allowing small errors to prop-agate undetected. Making temporal structure explicit via step-wise rationales enables early error detection and ef-ficient recovery. Together, these challenges highlight that deep reasoning paired with explicit visual sketches is es-sential for reliable manipulation in dynamic environments. Recent Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models [7–9, 19, 30, 52, 74] have made notable progress in mapping observations and language directly to actions. However, because plan intent is largely embedded in latent repre-sentations, these models struggle with task decomposition and causal explanation, both of which are critical for long-horizon operation in dynamic scenes. Hierarchical VLAs [61, 65, 77] attempt to address these challenges with plan-ner and controller systems, yet their reasoning is often in-stantaneous and lacks persistent modeling of global intent 

(e.g. evolving human goals, emerging errors, and prior states); they also provide limited support for referential dis-ambiguation , leaving spatial references unresolved in clut-tered environments. A complementary direction introduces 

think before act reasoning within VLAs, for example EO-1 [62] with interleaved vision, thinking, and action pretrain-ing, and OneTwoVLA [44] with adaptive joint reasoning and acting, but when the intermediate evidence remains text only , the spatial referential disambiguation behind actions (contact points, approach directions, object relations) stays implicit, which hinders human verification and deprives the controller of low-entropy geometric guidance. To address these, we externalize spatial intent at the lan-guage to control interface by introducing an explicit vi-sual intermediate representation that we call Visual Sketch ,which is a sparse set of action oriented primitives rendered on the current view or views: points (key contact or aim locations), bounding boxes (object or region selection), and 

arrows (desired motion or force directions). Co ground-ing language with these geometric sketches provides dis-ambiguation of referents and spatial constraints, improves supervisability via simple annotation or auto labeling, and enables interpretability and debuggability because humans can read, approve, or modify intent before execution. In ef-fect, the sketch serves as a verifiable contract between high level reasoning and low level control. Built upon Visual Sketch, we propose Action-Sketcher ,a VLA framework that operates in a See → Think →

Sketch → Act loop, as also shown in Fig. 1. Given multi-view observations and a task instruction, the model alter-nates among (1) Think , which produces a concise ratio-nale with temporal planning and spatial conception; (2) 

Sketch , which emits a scene- and subtask-tailored Visual Sketch (i.e., points, boxes and arrows); and (3) Act , which synthesizes a low-latency action chunk conditioned on the current observations. To enable adaptive switching and event-aware control, an adaptive token-gated strategy or-chestrates the loop, e.g., <BOR> (begin-of-reasoning) ini-tiates or refreshes global reasoning, and <BOA> (begin-of-action) triggers action-chunk prediction. The policy transi-tions among these modes based on observed state, predicted risk, and user feedback, thereby supporting reactive cor-rections and human interaction while preserving real-time 22434 action prediction. To support scalable training and evalu-ation, we curate a corpus that couples interleaved images, text, and action sequences with Visual Sketch supervision across diverse manipulation skills, cluttered tabletops, and multi-object scenes. Our multi-stage curriculum training recipe combines (i) foundational spatio-temporal learning 

for modality unification; (ii) language-to-sketch consistency 

to ensure precise phrase-to-geometry bindings ( e.g. “grasp the handle” → handle keypoint + approach arrow in the Vi-sual Sketch ); and (iii) imitation learning augmented with 

sketch-to-action reinforcement for robustness. The contri-butions of this paper are summarized as follows: • We formalize Visual Sketch as a co-grounded, explicit spatial-intent interface that renders points, boxes and arrows, thereby disambiguating where and how to act and serving as a human-verifiable contract between high-level reasoning and low-level control. • We propose Action-Sketcher , a VLA framework that operates in a See → Think → Sketch → Act loop, or-chestrated by token-gated states for adaptive switch-ing among reasoning, visual-sketch generation/revi-sion, and action synthesis, enabling real-time interrup-tion handling, error detection, and sketch-level correc-tion without sacrificing latency. • We curate an interleaved corpus and a training recipe that align language, Visual Sketches , and actions via in-terleaved sequence alignment, language-to-sketch con-sistency, and imitation learning with sketch-to-action reinforcement; empirically, the resulting system im-proves long-horizon success, robustness to dynamic scene changes, and interpretability, validated through comprehensive ablations and real-robot studies. 

## 2. Related Work 

Vision–Language–Action Models (VLAs). VLAs con-nect multimodal perception to low-level control on top of multimodal LLM backbones. End-to-end policies em-ploy regression-based [10, 24, 30, 36, 39, 50], diffusion-based [9, 40–42, 52, 75], or hybrid mappings [51] from ob-servations and language to actions, achieving strong short-horizon performance but often keeping plan intent latent, limiting task decomposition and causal explanation . Hi-erarchical VLAs [8, 19, 49, 61, 65, 74, 85] introduce planner–controller splits to improve long-horizon behav-ior; however, reasoning is frequently instantaneous or lo-cal and lacks persistent modeling of global intent ( e.g. 

evolving human goals, emerging errors, and prior states), which hampers human-in-the-loop interaction and referen-tial disambiguation in dynamic scenes. Recent think be-fore act variants integrate explicit reasoning within a uni-fied backbone, such as EO-1 [62] and O NE TWO VLA [44]. THINK ACT [25] further couples textual plans with a re-inforced visual plan latent to condition downstream ac-tion models, improving long-horizon planning; yet the plan is ultimately compressed into a latent that is not human-editable, limiting persistent, verifiable intent during execu-tion. This motivates an interface that makes intent observ-able and actionable. 

Robotic visual prompting, sketches, and traces. Vi-sual prompting encodes action intent via geometric or pic-torial cues rendered on the image plane. At the representa-tion level, LLARVA [58] predicts two-dimensional traces to align vision and action spaces, and R OBO BRAIN [28, 73] emits explicit visual guidance ( e.g. pointing, affor-dances, trajectories) for downstream policies. At the pol-icy level, RT-T RAJECTORY [22] conditions on rough trajec-tory sketches, RT-S KETCH [69] leverages hand-drawn goal sketches under ambiguity and distractors, T RACE VLA [84] injects visual trace prompting, and R OVI [37] systematizes object-centric symbols (arrows, circles, colors, numbers). Contemporaneously, MOLMO ACT [34] generates depth-aware tokens and mid-level spatial plans as editable trajec-tory traces to steer actions. While these works show the value of explicit visual cues, they either freeze prompts as static inputs or compress plans into non-editable latents. In contrast, our Visual Sketch is persistent, human-editable, and produced one sub-task at a time, providing targeted and scene-aligned guidance. Because sketches no longer encode the entire trajectory, they can express richer and more pre-cise action primitives such as contact keypoints, rotation ar-rows, and placement cues instead of coarse traces. 

## 3. Method 

Our proposed framework, Action-Sketcher, is designed to address the challenges of spatial ambiguity and temporal brittleness in long-horizon manipulation tasks. By intro-ducing an explicit visual intermediate, we create a verifi-able and correctable interface between high-level reason-ing and low-level action execution. This section first details the structure and semantics of our proposed Visual Sketch 

in Sec.3.1. We then present the complete Action–Sketcher 

framework in Sec.3.2, encompassing the problem formula-tion, the dual-mode inference pipeline, and the multi-stage curriculum learning strategy.

3.2. Action Sketcher Framework 

Our framework formulates long-horizon manipulation as a sequence modeling task over a hybrid output space ( i.e., dis-crete and continuous). We aim to learn a policy πθ that maps context to a joint output distribution, enabling the agent to seamlessly interleave discrete reasoning (for planning and sketching) and continuous action prediction, autonomously deciding the appropriate mode at each timestep. 

3.2.1. See-Think-Sketch-Act Pipeline 

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the model’s input context is a se-quence of tokens representing multi-view images ( e.g. left-wrist, right-wrist, base), the task instruction, a history of completed subtasks, the current subtask, and the visual sketch image. Action-Sketcher operates in two distinct, adaptively switched modes: Reasoning Mode and Action Mode . This dual-mode design allows the model to dynam-ically balance the need for careful deliberation with the de-mand for real-time execution. The switch between modes is governed by a token-gated mechanism. 

(1) Reasoning Mode. If the model determines that rea-soning is required ( e.g. after completing a subtask, encoun-tering an error, or receiving human intervention), it gen-erates a special token, <BOR> (begin-of-reasoning). Fol-lowing this, the model auto-regressively performs temporal and spatial reasoning. Temporal Reasoning: The model first analyzes the current scene, considering the overall task in-struction and the history of completed subtasks to deduce the next logical subtask. Spatial Reasoning: Based on the newly identified subtask, the model performs spatial reason-ing about the object layout and relationships in the scene. This process yields the text-based elements for the Visual Sketch (points, boxes, arrows) that correspond to the sub-task. This reasoning phase concludes with the generation of an <EOR> (end-of-reasoning) token. The generated text-based sketch is then rendered onto the current reference view to create an image-based Visual Sketch . The input con-text is subsequently updated with the new subtask and the new sketch image. 

(2) Action Mode. Conversely, if the model deems rea-soning unnecessary, such as during the routine execution of a subtask where the scene remains consistent, it generates a <BOA> (begin-of-action) token. This triggers the action expert to generate action chunks via flow-matching [45]. Initially, the context tokens for completed subtasks, the current subtask, and the visual sketch image are empty. The model must begin in Reasoning Mode to populate these fields. Subsequently, it can fluidly switch between modes to maintain both accuracy and real-time performance. 

3.2.2. Model Structure 

The Action-Sketcher framework is model-agnostic and can be integrated with any VLA model. For a specific instance, we employ π0 [9] as the backbone. The model is trained to auto-regressively generate textual reasoning chains, subtask plans, and structured descriptions of visual sketches, while simultaneously optimizing a flow-matching loss [45] to pre-dict continuous action chunks. Details for model structure can refer to Appendix A.1. 

3.2.3. Training Strategy 

To realize the “See-Think-Sketch-Act” pipeline, we em-ploy a multi-stage curriculum learning strategy. This cur-riculum is designed to first establish a broad foundation of spatiotemporal understanding (Stage 1), then specialize this knowledge to align high-level language with our structured 

Visual Sketch representation (Stage 2), and finally, to jointly train the action policy and the adaptive mode-switching mechanism for robust, real-world execution (Stage 3). We elaborate on each stage below. 

Stage 1: Foundational Spatiotemporal Learning. The initial stage focuses on pre-training the model to develop general spatiotemporal modeling and reasoning capabili-ties. We utilize a large-scale dataset for this purpose. For spatial understanding, the training data comprises 3.4M samples from diverse sources, including Visual Ground-ing datasets [23, 28, 33, 35] for object-level bounding box prediction, Spatial Pointing datasets [17, 73, 79, 82, 86] for keypoint prediction, Scene Understanding datasets [4, 54, 78] for broader scene perception, and general visual question-answering (VQA) datasets [47, 48, 48]. For tem-poral learning, we construct a dataset of 870k sequences 22437 by integrating data from EgoPlan [15], ShareRobot [28], and AgiBot-World [11]. To foster explicit reasoning, we annotate 20% of this corpus with detailed textual ratio-nales generated by GPT-4o [27]. This stage equips the Action-Sketcher with robust spatiotemporal reasoning and instruction-following abilities. 

Stage 2: Reasoning-to-Sketch Enhancement. In this stage, the model learns the coherent reasoning process re-quired for the Reasoning Mode. The objective is to ana-lyze a scene and, guided by the task instruction and execu-tion history, perform sequential temporal and spatial rea-soning to generate the next subtask and its correspond-ing text-based Visual Sketch . The training data is curated from 2.6k episodes of diverse, long-horizon (with 2–16 sub-tasks) manipulation tasks in complex layouts (over 20 ob-ject types with various spatial arrangements) that we col-lected in the real world, encompassing tasks such as tidy-ing a tabletop, pouring tea, and general pick-and-place sce-narios. We further supplement this by annotating 1.7k full trajectories from existing datasets, including LIBERO [46] and RoboTwin 2.0 [14]. This culminates in a reasoning-to-sketch dataset of 21k samples used to fine-tune the Action-Sketcher to master the complete Reasoning Mode pipeline. 

Stage 3: Sketch-to-Action and Mode Adaptation. The final stage jointly trains the action policy and the mode-switching mechanism. The training data serves two pur-poses. First, it teaches adaptive mode selection by provid-ing examples where a <BOR> token is appropriate ( e.g. at subtask boundaries or upon scene changes) versus a <BOA> 

token (during nominal execution). Second, it trains the ac-tion expert using action-labeled data. To enhance robust-ness, we augment the Visual Sketch corresponding to each action label to simulate potential inference-time inaccura-cies. Specifically, for bounding boxes , we apply random perturbations to the ground-truth box while maintaining an Intersection over Union (IoU) of at least 0.8. For points , we randomly sample new coordinates within a circle of a small radius c around the ground-truth point. Arrows are adjusted according to points . This augmentation ensures the action expert is resilient to minor errors in the generated sketch. Furthermore, we address the significant data imbalance inherent in task execution, where Action Mode steps far out-number Reasoning Mode steps. To prevent the model from developing a bias towards the more frequent <BOA> token, we employ a mode-balanced sampling strategy. Let DR and

## 4. Experiments 

We conduct extensive experiments on both simulated envi-ronments and a real-world robotic platform to validate the effectiveness of Action-Sketcher in long-horizon, complex manipulation tasks. Our evaluation is structured around three key research questions: • RQ1: How does the performance of Action-Sketcher, across tasks of varying temporal and spatial complexity, compare to state-of-the-art baseline models? • RQ2: What are the primary failure modes of our system, and how effectively can human-in-the-loop intervention, via the Visual Sketch interface, improve the success rate? • RQ3: How do the core components of the Action-Sketcher framework affect the performance? 

4.1. Task Performance Evaluation (RQ1) 

Experiment Settings. We first evaluate the overall task suc-cess rate of Action-Sketcher against a suite of VLA base-lines. Our evaluation is performed in two simulated bench-marks and on a real-world robotic arm. For simulation, we use LIBERO [46], a benchmark focused on lifelong skills, and an enhanced version of RoboTwin 2.0 [14] with increased object clutter and spatial complexity, including 

“stack three blocks” , “hanging mugs” , “place empty cups” 

and “place a2b anywhere” . For the real world, we evalu-ate on three long-horizon tasks: “tidying a cluttered table-top” , “pouring tea” , and “general pick-and-place” with ambiguous instructions, built upon both Agilex and Galaxea dual-arm robot platform. We report the average success rate across all tasks in each benchmark suite.

## 5. Conclusion 

In this work, we presented Action-Sketcher, a VLA frame-work designed to address spatial ambiguity and temporal brittleness in long-horizon robotic manipulation. To achieve this, we introduced Visual Sketch , an explicit spatial repre-sentation that operationalizes the reasoning process through a structured See-Think-Sketch-Act cycle. By adaptively switching between reasoning to generate interpretable vi-sual plans and executing actions conditioned on them, our approach outperforms state-of-the-art methods, particularly in tasks requiring complex spatial grounding and multi-step sequential planning. Extensive experiments and ablations further demonstrate that Visual Sketch is the pivotal com-ponent, providing a robust bridge between high-level rea-soning and low-level control. Its explicit nature not only improves autonomous performance but also enables effec-tive human-in-the-loop correction, paving the way for more transparent and collaborative robotic agents.
