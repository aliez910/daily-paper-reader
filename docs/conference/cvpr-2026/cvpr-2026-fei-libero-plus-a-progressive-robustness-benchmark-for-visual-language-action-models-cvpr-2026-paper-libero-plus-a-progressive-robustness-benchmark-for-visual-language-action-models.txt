## Abstract 

Visual–Language–Action (VLA) models report impressive success rates exceeding 95% on robotic manipulation benchmarks, yet these results may mask fundamental weak-nesses in robustness. Current simulation-based robust-ness evaluations suffer from narrow perturbation cover-age, manual design constraints, and coarse-grained analy-sis that fails to reveal when and how models fail. To address this gap, we propose LIBERO-Plus, a comprehensive, au-tomatic, and fine-grained evaluation framework with con-trolled perturbations across seven dimensions: object lay-outs, camera viewpoints, robot initial states, language in-structions, lighting conditions, background textures, and sensor noise. Our systematic analysis of ten state-of-the-art models reveals consistent brittleness beneath apparent competence, with performance dropping from 95% to below 30% under modest perturbations. Our findings challenge the assumption that high benchmark scores equate to true competency and highlight the need for evaluation practices that assess reliability under realistic variation. 

## 1. Introduction 

Recent advances in Visual–Language–Action (VLA) mod-els have achieved remarkable performance on embodied manipulation tasks [2, 5, 9, 11, 12, 14, 20, 22], with sys-tems reporting success rates exceeding 95–99% on popu-lar simulation benchmarks like LIBERO [16]. While these results suggest that manipulation might be close to being “solved” under current evaluation protocols, real-world de-ployment often reveals a stark contrast, with models strug-gling when faced with environmental variations such as lighting changes, shifts in camera viewpoints, or different

While simulation environments provide essential controlled settings for fair model com-parison and reproducible evaluation rather than costly real-world experimentation, current simulation-based evaluation practices focus primarily on performance under fixed, ideal conditions and fail to assess model robustness across the diverse variations that comprehensive simulation environ-ments can systematically provide. Existing simulation-based efforts to address robustness evaluation have introduced perturbations along specific axes, such as object variations, lighting changes, and para-phrased instructions [6, 7, 17, 21, 23, 26]. While valuable, these simulation-based initiatives have limitations in three key areas. First, they often focus on narrow perturbation types rather than providing a systematic evaluation across a broad spectrum of robustness factors that simulation en-vironments can controllably generate. Second, many rely on manually designed perturbations, which constrain scal-ability and reproducibility. Third, most evaluations report aggregate success rates, which mask the nuanced failures and make it difficult to pinpoint the exact conditions under which a model fails. As a result, current simulation-based approaches offer fragmented insights but lack the method-ological rigor to systematically characterize the boundaries of model capabilities or guide targeted improvements. In this work, we introduce LIBERO-Plus, a systematic framework for progressive robustness evaluation of end-to-end VLA models. Building on the widely adopted LIBERO benchmark, our framework extends evaluation along three methodological directions. First, we provide comprehen-sive perturbation coverage across seven major categories and fourteen subcategories, including camera viewpoints, robot initial states, language instructions, lighting condi-tions, background textures, sensor noise, and object lay-outs, moving beyond narrow perturbation types to system-atic evaluation across a broad spectrum of robustness fac-tors. ## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Vision-Language-Action Models 

Recent advancements in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have expanded the paradigm of foundation mod-els from language and vision into robotics, motivating uni-fied architectures. Autoregressive approaches [3, 11, 14, 15, 20, 24] discretize robot actions into tokens and train end-to-end policies on large-scale demonstrations, while diffusion-based models [1, 2, 13, 25] generate continuous trajecto-ries via generative diffusion experts. More recently, rein-forcement learning methods [8, 17, 18, 22] move beyond supervised fine-tuning, emphasizing robustness and down-stream adaptability through reinforcement learning objec-tives. However, while these models show “zero-shot” per-formance in familiar settings, their success often reflects in-terpolation rather than true generalization. As a result, ex-isting benchmarks lack comprehensive insights into model performance under distribution shifts, highlighting the need for systematic and fine-grained robustness evaluations.

## 3. LIBERO-Plus 

LIBERO-Plus is a comprehensive benchmark for systemat-ically evaluating VLA model generalization in robotic ma-nipulation. It extends LIBERO with 10,030 task instances across 7 perturbation factors and 21 sub-dimensions. 

3.1. Benchmark Construction 

We build on LIBERO for its diverse manipulation scenar-ios with language instructions and multi-view observations. Perturbation factors are environmental and task properties systematically varied to induce distribution shifts. ## 4. How Do Single-Dimension Perturbations Af-fect VLA Models? 

We present the main experimental results in Table 2 and Figure 3, which collectively reveal a significant fragility in the generalization capabilities of current VLAs. As shown, even minor perturbations can lead to drastic performance degradation. Below we analyze the specific robustness pat-terns across perturbation dimensions, models, and tasks. 

Finding 1: Significant Overall Fragility to Perturba-tions. Across all perturbation factors, current VLAs exhibit brittle generalization. Performance degrades significantly under various input perturbations, particularly with changes in camera viewpoint and robot initial state. 

Finding 2: Robustness varies considerably by per-turbation type. Models are most vulnerable to changes in camera viewpoint and robot initial state, which require a high-level understanding of spatial geometry and proprio-ception. In contrast, they show relative resilience to lighting and background variations, which constitute more superfi-cial, low-level visual changes. 

Finding 3: Minor Impact of Language Perturbation. 

Contrary to expectations, language perturbations result in the second smallest performance drop (-25.3) on average, a counter-intuitive result that warrants further investigation. As discussed in Section 6, this likely does not stem from better linguistic generalization. Instead, as empirically vali-dated, a more plausible explanation is that models may rely less on language instructions than expected, possibly by ex-tracting task cues from visual context. 

Finding 4: Model robustness is dictated by architec-ture and training paradigm. Specifically, models incor-porating a first-person wrist camera (e.g., OpenVLA-OFT )demonstrate superior generalization, especially to camera viewpoint changes, compared to those reliant solely on a third-person view (e.g., OpenVLA-OFT w ). ## 6. Do Contemporary VLA Models Truly Fol-low Language Instructions? 

In the experiments presented in Section 4, we observed an intriguing phenomenon: when introducing language per-turbations, the overall performance of the OpenVLA-OFT model was barely affected and remained close to the base-line level. To further investigate the potential underlying reasons, we propose the following three hypotheses: 

(1) The model may possess strong generalization capa-bilities in the language domain , allowing it to remain robust even when instructions are perturbed. 

(2) The model may extract limited keywords from the in-put instruction for matching and decision-making , rather than genuinely understanding the full semantic structure. However, this is unlikely because our perturbations include a commonsense subclass that performs keyword common-sense rewrite, yet the performance drop remains nearly neg-ligible. 

(3) The model may not fully utilize the language modal-ity, instead relying primarily on visual or other non-linguistic signals to complete tasks. In such a scenario, language inputs would be functionally redundant, and even significant perturbations would have minimal impact. To verify which of the above hypotheses is more plausi-ble, we conducted additional analysis experiments. 

6.1. What If We Remove Language, Does Perfor-mance Drop? 

We introduced a blank instruction experiment. In this set-ting, the language input provided to the model was entirely replaced with an empty value, i.e., no linguistic informa-tion was supplied during inference. This approach directly tests whether the absence of language leads to a substan-tial performance degradation. We conducted experiments on all four suites of LIBERO, and the results are shown in Figure 6(a). Surprisingly, even without any valid language input, the performance of OpenVLA-OFT on the object suite re-mained largely unchanged, with significant degradation ob-served only on the long suite. We attribute this to the greater reliance on instruction guidance in long-horizon tasks, which forces the model to attend to the language modality. This finding is highly revealing: although the model is nominally designed as a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework, in practice it degenerates into a form that disregards language, behaving more like a Vision-Action (VA) model. 

6.2. What If We Replace Goals with OOD Objects, Do Models Fail? 

We further designed a goal replacement task to di-rectly examine whether models genuinely possess language instruction-following ability. Specifically, for the layout 

suite, where the issue appeared most pronounced, we re-placed the target object in the instruction and the task goal with alternatives within the same scene. As shown in Fig-ure 6(b), the experimental results revealed two key findings: 

Finding 7: VLA models do not possess strong cross-object instruction-following generalization . In tasks with replaced targets, the model’s success rate dropped nearly to zero, with the degradation particularly severe for OpenVLA-OFT. The apparent “robustness” observed in prior language perturbation experiments did not stem from a deep modeling of language but rather from ignoring linguis-tic inputs altogether, leading to a superficially stable perfor-mance under perturbations. 

Finding 8: VLA models appear to rely more on fixed vision–action mappings than on fully exploiting lan-guage signals in task decision-making . By analyzing roll-out cases, we observed that even when the target in the in-struction was explicitly changed, the model still tended to execute the original target action rather than adjust its be-havior according to the new instruction. We perform a total of 30k independent repeated experi-ments across 6 different perturbation (excluding language) combinations using the OpenVLA-OFT model to ensure high statistical significance. We perform independent tests for each type of single-dimension perturbation and pairwise perturbations, with 2000 trials conducted for each dimen-sion combination. Finding 9: Generalization is intrinsically non-decomposable . The findings indicate that current models lack mecha-nisms to capture higher-order dependencies, leading to pro-nounced robustness degradation under complex perturba-tion combinations.

## 9. Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced LIBERO-Plus, a systematic framework for progressive robustness evaluation that pro-vides comprehensive perturbation coverage across 7 pertur-bation, automated generation of over 56K robustness sce-narios, and fine-grained analysis through progressive diffi-culty stratification. Evaluation of 10 popular VLA mod-els reveals a significant generalization gap, contrasting their near-saturated performance on benchmarks like LIBERO. Most VLA models remain brittle, showing particular vul-nerability to camera and robot state changes, almost all models ignore the language instructions, and some of the models execute with a bare memorization of the trajectory instead of relying on visual feedback. We also identify po-sitional bias and negative combinatorial generalization gaps under combined perturbations.
