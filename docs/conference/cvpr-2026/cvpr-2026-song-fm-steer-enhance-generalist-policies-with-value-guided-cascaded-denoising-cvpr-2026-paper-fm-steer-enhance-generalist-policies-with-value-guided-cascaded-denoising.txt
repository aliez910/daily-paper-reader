# Abstract 

Humans naturally allocate more time before acting when handling complex tasks in the physical world. This paradigm has recently led to remarkable advances in boost-ing Large Language Models (LLMs) on complex tasks in digital domains. However, the potential of test-time comput-ing remains largely unexplored for robotic foundation mod-els that interact with the physical world. In this work, we propose FM-Steer , a test-time computing framework that augments flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) gen-eralist policies with value-guided sampling and cascaded

FM-Steer first introduces an intermediate flow ver-ifier to estimate state-action values for candidate actions. At test time, the policy iteratively samples multiple noisy action proposals and retains the one with the highest pre-dicted value, yielding value-aligned, high-quality actions without retraining. To satisfy the stringent frequency de-mands of robot control, FM-Steer further introduces cas-caded action denoising, decoupling expensive value-guided sampling from fast action refinement. A lightweight Lite-Flow denoiser asynchronously takes the selected high-value noisy action and rapidly denoises it to produce the final con-trol signal, enabling fluid, high-rate execution. # 1. Introduction 

Creating generalist robots to perform various tasks in the physical world has been a long-standing goal [4, 5, 30, 52, 54, 59, 65]. Cognitive psychologists have revealed that hu-mans allocate more time when tackling complex problems, such as mathematical proofs or decision-making. Inspired by this paradigm, Large Language Models (LLMs) have shifted from purely train-time scale-up to test-time scal-ing, where allocating extra compute at evaluation. [17, 18, 28, 86] Specifically, repeated sampling and selection yield notable improvements across multiple domains, including chat, mathematics, and coding, suggesting that analogous test-time scaling in VLAs could deliver similar gains. How-ever, robot control has stricter real-time requirements than text generation: extra inference computation can introduce delays, causing jitter or even task failure. [2, 21, 22, 25, 60– 62, 69] Therefore, an essential question is: How can we enhance existing VLAs in test time while preserving the efficiency required for real-time robotic control? 

Some efforts have integrated text-based chain-of-thought or reasoning [43, 73, 78, 87, 88] into VLA models [7, 36, 71, 77], aiming to guide action generation with the world knowledge encoded in pretrained VLMs. [35, 38, 70] More-over, V-GPS [50] and RoboMonkey [33] have shown that “repeat-sampling and verification” can be an effective test-time scaling strategy for vision-language-action models: the model proposes multiple candidate actions, and an indepen-dent verifier chooses the best one. However, these attempts all focus on autoregressive VLA models and do not exploit flow-based VLA models, which show impressive dexterous robot control by leveraging the strong generative capacity of flow matching. [37, 45, 64, 67, 72] Additionally, autore-gressive text decoding and repeat-sampling significantly in-crease inference time, which in turn reduces the control fre-quency, making the policy unable to perform dexterous and dynamic tasks. [68, 76, 79, 80] Therefore, two key questions arise: how can we perform effective test-time computing for flow-based VLA models, and how can we maintain or even improve the efficiency required for real-time robot control? To address these challenges, we introduce FM-Steer, a framework that enhances flow-based VLA models at test time with value-guided test-time sampling and cascaded ac-tion denoising. Value-guided test-time sampling follows the “repeat-sampling and verification” paradigm. We first in-troduce noise forcing to mitigate the determinism of flow matching and repeatedly sample diverse intermediate noisy action candidates, and then attach an intermediate flow ver-ifier to the original VLA for verification. The intermedi-ate flow verifier is trained with offline reinforcement learn-ing and serves as a state-action value estimator, which is used to select the candidates with the highest value. Sim-ply applying repeated sampling at test time results in a low control frequency, which is impractical for dexterous robot control. To address this issue, we propose a cascaded action denoising mechanism that distributes the denoising compu-tation across the original VLA and a separate Lite-Flow de-noiser, a lightweight transformer designed to complete the remaining flow-matching process asynchronously. Through asynchronous inference, the original VLA performs value-guided test-time sampling at a low frequency (4 Hz), while the Lite-Flow denoiser continuously denoises the selected noisy action and predicts fluid actions in real time (90 Hz). In summary, the main contributions of this work are: • We propose FM-Steer, a test-time computing framework that enhances flow-based Vision-Language-Action mod-els while improving the robot control frequency. • We introduce value-guided test-time sampling and cas-caded action denoising for flow-matching action ex-perts. By iteratively sampling intermediate noisy ac-tions rather than fully denoised actions, part of the flow-matching computation in the original action expert is asynchronously offloaded to the Lite-Flow denoiser. • FM-Steer improves flow-based VLA models on multiple benchmarks and real-robot tests, achieving state-of-the-art performance. Powered by FM-Steer, π0 gains +4.4% on the LIBERO [44] benchmark, +25.9% on the Simpler benchmark [39], and +12.9% in real-world deployments. 

# 2. Related Work 

Flow-based Vision-Language-Action Models. Recently, several studies [5, 30, 48, 53, 56, 58, 84] have extended VLMs for robot control. RT-2 [5] fine-tunes PaLI-X [8] with discretized action tokens, while OpenVLA [30] adapts Prismatic VLM [29] on the OXE dataset [9]. These au-toregressive models generate actions by adding discretized action tokens into the VLM vocabulary. However, their dis-crete nature limits the model’s ability to perform dexterous motions. To model more refined physical behaviors, flow matching (a variant of diffusion) was introduced into ac-tion generation. As representative flow-based VLA models, 

π0 [4] and GR00T N1 [3] employ flow matching to predict action chunks in future time, achieving better performance on robot control than previous autoregressive models. 

Test-Time Scaling via Repeated Sampling. Numerous studies [20, 23, 47, 57, 66, 74, 81] have achieved remark-able success by applying additional computation at test time 13408 across various domains [13, 14, 51]. Through repeated sampling, large language models have achieved remark-able improvements in various tasks, including mathemat-ics, programming, and text summarization. For robotics, V-GPS [50] trains a separate action verifier to rank candi-date actions, preliminarily proving that repeated sampling can effectively enhance the generation of low-level robot actions. RoboMonkey [33] introduces a scalable data cura-tion pipeline and model architecture for training the action verifier. However, they do not explore stronger flow-based VLAs, which cannot generate diverse candidates through naive repeated sampling. Moreover, these models suffer from severe efficiency issues, making it difficult to perform highly dynamic and dexterous robot control tasks. 

Cascaded Denoising. Cascaded Denoising [24, 82, 83] was first proposed as a diffusion model [11, 12, 63] that generates higher-resolution images [15, 16, 75, 85] through a cascading approach. Starting with a diffusion model at low resolution, it continuously upsamples the gener-ated images to obtain high-resolution results. f-DM [19] applies inter-stage transformations for progressive signal restoration through function learning. Cas-DM [1] cascades noise-prediction and image-prediction modules to integrate perceptual losses in diffusion models. SCDM [6] cas-cades generation across spectral dimensions, reconstruct-ing hyperspectral bands progressively. HiFI [26] cascades consistent-resolution patches for memory-efficient high-resolution frame interpolation. CDM-VTON [34] employs two-stage cascading for virtual try-on applications. While these approaches have advanced capabilities in image do-mains, [40–42, 46, 55] the application of cascaded denois-ing for action generation remains largely unexplored.

# 4. Methodology 

In this section, we describe FM-Steer’s working pipeline in detail. As shown in Fig. 2, we take a flow-based VLA as the base model and augment it with two modules: an intermediate flow verifier φ and a Lite-Flow denoiser ϕ.

4.1. Value-Guided Test-Time Sampling 

Due to the multiple Euler forward iterations required by flow-based VLA during inference, repeated action sampling leads to a significant increase in computational cost. To ad-dress this, we introduce value-guided test-time sampling. Instead of sampling the final actions as in previous meth-ods [48, 50], value-guided test-time sampling samples the flow points Aτn 

> t

(i.e., intermediate noisy actions) along the Euler forward trajectory. After identifying the most valu-able flow point, it is passed to the Lite-Flow denoiser (a light-weight action expert detailed in Sec. 4.2) to complete the remaining Euler forward steps. By discovering promis-ing flow points earlier in the flow matching process, value-guided test-time sampling substantially reduces the test-time computational overhead of the original VLA.

4.3. Training and Deployment Strategy 

Although FM-Steer contains two separate modules, the in-termediate flow verifier and the Lite-Flow denoiser, they can be trained simultaneously to improve training efficiency because their datasets can be built from the same robot demonstration dataset. The dataset for the intermediate flow verifier is constructed using the reward function described in Sec. 4.1. The action of the dataset used to train the Lite-Flow denoiser is obtained by sampling from the frozen orig-inal VLA, and the observation is obtained from the same robot demonstration dataset, as described in Sec. 4.2. During inference, the intermediate flow verifier and the Lite-Flow denoiser cooperate asynchronously to boost the overall control frequency. For example, at the ini-tial timestep t, the original VLA generates N = 5 candi-date action chunks {Aτ1

# 5. Experiments 

Our experiments evaluate whether FM-Steer can effectively enhance existing flow-based VLAs at test time while pre-serving the efficiency required for real-time robotic control. We study FM-Steer across diverse simulated and real-world robotic platforms, including humanoid robots, and compare it with previous state-of-the-art generalist policies, includ-ing prior test-time computing frameworks. Specifically, our experiments aim to answer the following questions: 1. How effectively can FM-Steer improve the performance of flow-based VLAs on diverse manipulation tasks? 2. To what extent does FM-Steer improve the efficiency of flow-based VLAs when performing test-time computing? 3. How do value-guided test-time sampling and cascaded action denoising contribute to the improvements in per-formance and efficiency? (Ablation studies) To answer these questions, as shown in Fig. 3, we evalu-ate FM-Steer across a diverse range of representative robot learning scenarios, including 3 simulation benchmarks and 3 different real-world robotic platforms, covering 15 robot learning scenarios and 21 real-world manipulation tasks.

5.2. Performance Enhancement Evaluation Setups and Baselines. To assess FM-Steer’s ability to enhance the performance of flow-based VLAs on diverse manipulation tasks, we employ three evaluation se-tups (two simulated and one real-robot), each probing a dif-ferent aspect of FM-Steer. First, to evaluate the absolute performance that FM-Steer can achieve, we build FM-Steer on two flow-based VLAs in LIBERO: π0 [4] and GR00T N1 [3], and compare them with the latest state-of-the-art generalist manipulation policies, including OpenVLA-OFT [31], OpenHelix [10], π0.5 [27], and π0 [4]. Sec-ond, to evaluate how much improvement FM-Steer brings to flow-based VLAs, we compare FM-Steer with two previous test-time computing methods, V-GPS [50] and RoboMon-key [33], alongside their base model OpenVLA [30] in SimplerEnv. Third, to evaluate whether FM-Steer can help flow-based VLAs generalize to real-world robots, we com-pare FM-Steer with both existing state-of-the-art general-ist manipulation policies and previous test-time computing methods on the real-world WidowX 250s robot platform.

On Google Robot tasks, FM-Steer also achieves state-of-the-art performance with an average suc-cess rate of 76.6% on the visual matching tasks and 60.3% on the visual aggregation tasks, demonstrating its strong ca-pability in handling the out-of-distribution settings.

Ablation Settings. To effectively validate the role of each module in FM-Steer’s framework, we set up four ablation settings. In setting [1]. w/o Cascaded Denoising, the noise level bound T is set to 1, meaning the original VLA will complete the entire denoising process, leading to all candi-dates being sampled from the same distribution and conse-quently reduces the range of candidates that the intermedi-ate flow verifier can choose from. In setting [2]. w/o Repeat Sampling, we sample only one flow point from the original VLA to test whether our candidate flow point generation effectively improves the model’s performance during infer-ence. In setting [3]. w/o Lite-Flow Denoiser, the Lite-Flow Denoiser is removed, and noisy actions selected from the original VLA are directly used to control the robot. In set-ting [4]. w/o Flow Verifier, we replace the flow verifier with a random strategy that randomly selects a flow point sam-pled from the original VLA, which tests whether the flow verifier can effectively choose the most valuable flow point.

# 6. Conclusion 

In this paper, we present FM-Steer, a test-time comput-ing framework that enhances flow-based Vision-Language-Action models at test time. FM-Steer combines value-guided test-time sampling with effective best-of-N selec-tion and cascaded action denoising, integrating the original VLA with a lightweight denoiser to achieve rapid and fluid control. Through extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world robots, we show that FM-Steer outperforms current state-of-the-art models. The gains are especially clear on complex tasks that require failure recovery, high-lighting a promising direction for generalist robot policies. 13414 Acknowledgments
