# Abstract 

Humans intuitively rely on text and symbols inscribed on objects ( e.g . “PULL”, “Squeeze and Turn”) to perform tasks safely and correctly. In contrast, vision-language-action models excel at following external language com-mands, but remain largely unaware of this object-centric information. This capability is essential for reliable robotic operation, yet progress remains unmeasured due to the ab-sence of standardized benchmarks. To address this gap, we introduce INSIGHT Bench, a benchmark that formalizes the task of “in-situ guide grounding”. INSIGHT Bench provides a comprehensive taxonomy that evaluates how agents uti-lize diverse guide information, including action-direction cues and procedural instructions. It also includes a scalable simulation framework that procedurally generates tasks and programmatically links each visual guide to its correspond-ing physical constraint. We release both the benchmark and the resulting trajectory dataset to support future research. Our evaluation of recent VLA models reveals a critical lim-itation: their ability to ground in-situ guides is inconsistent and strongly dependent on the type of information. While models succeed on some guide categories, they frequently fail on others. However, performance improves substan-tially when the same information is provided as language instructions, indicating that in-situ guides could contribute to manipulation performance if VLAs were capable of inter-preting them. These findings underscore the need for further research on understanding and grounding in-situ guides.

# 2. Related Work 

## 2.1. Visual and Symbolic Guidance in Robotics 

The notion that robots should comprehend symbols is not new. In robotics navigation, a significant body of work has explored using signage for localization and mapping [22, 33, 40]. A recent work, Sign Language [1], argues that signs are a ubiquitous element of human environments that allow a robot to extend its perception beyond the current field of view. This is a powerful concept we build upon. However, these studies have predominantly focused on nav-igation, where signs guide large-scale movement, rather than on the fine-grained manipulation required to interact with an object based on its local guides. While some ap-proaches [21] use visual prompting to generate appropriate robot actions from virtual instructions created by a human user, these methods do not account for pre-existing, in-situ visual or symbolic guides that are an intrinsic part of the environment itself. 

## 2.2. Robot Action Models 

The advent of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models has reshaped the paradigm for robotic manipulation [4, 18, 29, 30, 34]. These models excel at mapping high-level, external language commands to generalizable actions by leveraging web-scale pre-training. However, their ability to compre-hend the fine-grained semantics of symbols and text phys-ically inscribed on objects—and to incorporate the result-ing constraints into action planning—remains largely un-verified. Our work directly addresses this gap by focusing on challenges that in-situ guides can proactively solve. For instance, identifying an object’s affordances—the possible interactions it allows—often requires complex learning from trial-and-error [12] or human demonstrations [2]. In-situ guides offer an explicit signal for these affor-dances, potentially bypassing these complex discovery pro-cesses. Similarly, manipulation is often complicated by pro-cedural constraints, such as needing to squeeze the bottle before rotate. The prevailing paradigm for handling this is post-hoc discovery, where a robot learns a recovery policy after an initial failure [39]. In-situ guides offer a proactive alternative. An instruction like “Squeeze and Turn” exter-nalizes the constraint, converting a reactive error-handling problem into one of direct instruction-following. In this context, in-situ guides can be viewed as a form of “asyn-chronous human intention, where the designer’s operational knowledge is embedded directly onto the object, providing clear guidance without requiring a human in the loop. 

## 2.3. Robotic Manipulation Benchmarks 

Numerous benchmarks have significantly advanced robotic manipulation, each focusing on different axes of gener-alization. For example, LIBERO [24] and CALVIN [27] challenge agents with long-horizon, language-conditioned tasks, while ManiSkill2 [15] promotes skill generaliza-tion across diverse objects and scenes. More recently, ARNOLD [13] was introduced to evaluate policy learning for tasks with continuous goal states ( e.g . “open the drawer 75%”), tackling the challenge of fine-grained control from external language instructions. However, these benchmarks all rely on instructions ex-ternal to the task environment, such as language commands 35071 Figure 2. INSIGHT Bench establishes this missing evaluation axis, focusing on the compositional reasoning needed to translate on-object visual information into physically constrained actions. 

# 3. In-Situ Guided Manipulation Problem 

In-Situ Guided Manipulation is defined as a robot’s abil-ity to interpret and execute manipulation tasks based on in-structions physically attached to the target object, such as printed labels or engraved symbols. Unlike prior approaches that rely on separate manuals or verbal commands, this form of guidance is co-located with the object and directly linked to its affordances. We refer to these instructions as In-Situ Guides . As illustrated in Fig. 1, In-Situ Guides include both symbolic cues and textual descriptions, providing informa-tion on how the object should be handled and where contact should be made. To analyze this guidance, we propose a tax-onomy comprising five categories: Contact Affordance In-formation , In-Part Contact Specification , Action Direc-tional Information , Procedural Guidance Information ,and Target Disambiguation Information .First, contact affordance information, signals which 

component is intended for interaction. By being located near or pointing to a specific part, the guide identifies the primary interaction point, especially if not geometrically obvious. For example, a “PUSH” text on a flat door panel or an arrow pointing to a small button identifies the target com-ponent. The second category, in-part contact specification ,details the precise contact manner or location on the part it-self, beyond part identification. We define ‘part’ as shared functional components across object categories ( e.g . han-dles, caps, buttons), following [11]. For instance, as shown in Fig. 2, the guide on a child-proof bottle indicates the spe-cific pressure points and orientation on the cap required to disengage the safety mechanism. The third category, action directional information, imposes constraints on manipula-tion execution. This prevents invalid actions by restricting the robot’s motion space. For example, an arrow on a bottle cap indicates the rotational direction, while a “PUSH” label on a door enforces a pushing action. The fourth category, 

procedural guidance , specifies the sequence for multi-step operations. For instance, labels such as “Rotate handle and Pull door” define the requisite order of actions as shown

in Fig. 1. Finally, target disambiguation resolves ambiguity when multiple candidate parts afford interaction. For exam-ple, as shown in Fig. 2, if several drawers are present but only the top drawer is marked, the guide specifies the target for a generic command like “open the drawer.” Based on this taxonomy, we construct a dataset and benchmark that instantiate these five categories of in-situ guidance across diverse manipulation scenarios. This benchmark serves as a standardized testbed for evaluating how effectively algorithms interpret and leverage in-situ guides. In the following section, we introduce the dataset and benchmark in detail. 

# 4. Insight Bench 

## 4.1. Simulation Environment 

INSIGHT Bench is built on NVIDIA’s Isaac Lab [28]. This framework utilizes GPU-parallelized physics and pho-torealistic rendering, enabling the generation of high-fidelity visual inputs essential for VLA models. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the setup features a fixed 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda arm with a parallel gripper. Perception relies on four cameras: three main cameras(two mounted above the robot’s left and right shoulders to observe the workspace, and one wrist-mounted camera) and one addi-tional camera for guide perception. The simulator provides RGB images (224 ×224), depth maps, camera intrinsics, and semantic segmentation masks for the guides. To ensure 35072 simulation fidelity, friction coefficients of object joints were empirically calibrated for each asset and task. Object assets, such as cabinets and doors, are sourced from the Isaac Sim library and prior datasets [11, 20, 39]. We refined these assets through convex decomposition, joint offset correction, and mesh simplification to ensure stable physics-based manipulation. Since guide assets ( e.g . arrows and text) are not natively supported, we developed a custom generation pipeline. We processed 12 symbolic icons from the Google Material Symbols Library 1 and created 20 text assets using Blender 2, exporting them to USD format. 

## 4.2. Task Design 

The INSIGHT Bench task suite is designed to evaluate agents across the reasoning dimensions defined in our tax-onomy. Each task isolates specific information types to test the agent’s comprehension capabilities. We present three tasks—Cabinet, Door, and Bottle—encompassing various primitive motions (e.g., rotating, grasping, pushing) and dis-tinct subsets of guidance information. Tab. 1 summarizes the alignment between tasks and informational dimensions, and Fig. 1 illustrates example scenes. 

4.2.1. Cabinet 

This task addresses the challenge of visual ambiguity in opening a designated door or drawer. A single asset often contains multiple interactive parts, making the target com-ponent unclear. To resolve this, each scenario places a single in-situ guide (an arrow) to uniquely identify the target, as shown in Fig. 2. This setup evaluates target disambiguation 

and contact affordance by requiring the agent to interpret the guide and select the correct handle joint among multiple candidates. The task is procedurally generated from 102 ob-ject assets ranging in complexity (2 to 6 targetable joints), resulting in a total of 267 target scenarios. 

4.2.2. Door 

The Door task features four configurations based on handle rotation (clockwise/counter-clockwise) and hinge mechan-ics (push/pull). To enforce the required procedural logic, a physical constraint is implemented in simulation: the hinge joint remains locked until the handle has been sufficiently rotated to release the latch mechanism. This task evaluates the agent’s ability to interpret contact affordance , action di-rectional information (via text or symbols) and follow pro-cedural guidance (i.e., rotate then push/pull). Each of the 55 

door assets can be configured with any of these four varia-tions, resulting in 220 scenes.

## 4.3. Success Criteria 

Task success is determined by whether a target joint exceeds a specified threshold. These thresholds are calibrated to pri-oritize the agent’s reasoning ability to interpret guides and resolve physical constraints. Since valid actuation is often physically impossible without first solving guide-imposed constraints ( e.g . unlocking a handle), successfully mov-ing the joint past a threshold serves as sufficient evidence of guide comprehension. Detailed threshold definitions for each task type are provided in the Supplementary Material.

4.4.1. Scene Generation 

As shown in Fig. 4, given specific task and variation, we randomly generate scenes by sampling object assets and their corresponding in-situ guides. The sampled guides are automatically spawned near the interaction point, while en-suring that the physical constraints of the sampled objects are aligned with the in-situ guides. The proposed genera-tion process consists of two key stages: • Stage 1. Guide-to-physics grounding: This stage en-forces a direct coupling between in-situ guides and phys-ical constraints. For each episode, we sample a high-level task ( e.g . ‘Bottle’) and a specific sub-task ( e.g . ‘squeeze and open clockwise’). A corresponding visual guide is rendered, and the associated physical constraint is pro-grammatically activated. For instance, in a Bottle Squeeze 

scenario, the cap’s revolute joint is locked unless the end-effector applies a squeezing force. Conversely, for a stan-dard ‘rotate’ sub-task, this constraint is inactive. This pro-grammatic grounding transforms the task from simple vi-sual pattern matching to causal reasoning. • Stage 2. Guide spatial randomization: To prevent over-fitting to specific spatial configurations, we decouple the guide’s semantic content from its placement. Our frame-work defines a semantically relevant region on the tar-get object ( e.g . the top surface of a bottle cap or drawer front panel). Within this region, the guide’s position and orientation are procedurally sampled. This randomization compels the agent to actively locate the guide and reason about its content, ensuring the evaluation of robust inter-pretation capabilities rather than spatial memorization. Finally, these guide-specific variations are layered atop general domain randomization, including diverse asset pose, masses, and friction coefficients. 

4.4.2. Trajectory Data Generation 

To generate a large-scale dataset of demonstrations, we em-ploy a hierarchical, skill-based pipeline. At the high level ,each task is defined by a fixed sequence of parameterized skills (e.g., G RASP → ROTATE → PULL ). At the low level ,the parameters θi for each skill ψi are programmatically in-stantiated based on the simulation state. For instance, in a cabinet-open task, the framework identifies the target han-35074 dle link and samples a grasp pose in its vicinity. Once parameterized, skills are executed via a motion planner, CuRobo [36] and tracked by a joint-level PID controller to ensure feasible trajectory tracking. A complete demonstration trajectory is then represented as  ζ = (ot, a t, r t) Tt=0 , where ot, at and rt denote obser-vations, actions, and rewards. The observation ot consists of both proprioceptive states (joint angles, velocities, grip-per status) and visual inputs (multi-view RGB images and segmentation masks). The action at ∈ R8 contains the tar-get joint positions of the 7-DoF arm and the gripper com-mand. The reward rt is sparse, evaluated only at the end of the episode to determine task success. Only trajectories satisfying the success criteria are retained in the dataset. The skill library S consists of three primitives:  S =

{GRASP (·), ROTATE (·), PULL (·)}. Each primitive is de-fined by a compact parameterization. The G RASP primitive, denoted as ψgrasp (p, q ), moves the end-effector to a specified target pose, where, p ∈ R3 is the 3D position and q ∈ H

is the orientation represented as a quaternion. This motion also incorporates an approach phase before gripping. The ROTATE primitive, ψrot (ϕ), rotates the end-effector about its local z-axis by angle ϕ (radians). If the motion encounters a joint limit, the controller resets and resumes until the ac-tion is completed. The P ULL primitive, ψpull (d), translates the end-effector by displacement d along its forward axis, where positive/negative d denotes pushing/pulling. 

4.4.3. Data Curation and Statistics 

All generated data is stored at a 10Hz frequency using the LeRobot [6] format. The choice of this format facilitates immediate usability for researchers, significantly lowering the barrier to entry for training and evaluating new models on INSIGHT Bench. The dataset is also enriched with skill-level labeling ( e.g . Grasp, Rotate) at each timestep, mak-ing it suitable for research in hierarchical learning and skill-based policies. To enable a controlled investigation into the role of in-situ guides, we collected a Guided Dataset and a Guideless Dataset. The Guided Dataset is our primary dataset, containing 14 , 076 demonstration trajectories for tasks where objects feature the visual guides as described. It is used for training policies that are expected to learn from and leverage these explicit cues. The Guideless Dataset is a baseline dataset for the same tasks and objects, but with-out spawning guide assets. It enables a direct, comparative analysis of policies trained with and without visual guid-ance, providing a crucial control condition.

## 5.1. Experimental Setup 

5.1.1. Baseline Models and Training Conditions 

We evaluate three VLA models: π0 [4], SmolVLA [34], and GR00T N1.5 [29]. All policies are trained as multi-task agents, learning all Cabinet , Door , and Bottle 

tasks simultaneously. The proprioceptive state inputs vary by model: while π0 and SmolVLA utilize only joint states, GR00T uses an expanded state representation that includes joint states, end-effector poses, joint velocities, and previ-ous actions. All three models share a structure of a VLM backbone and an action expert based on flow matching [8, 16, 23, 35]. For the VLM backbone, π0 uses PaliGemma (3B parameter) [3], SmolVLA uses SmolVLM-2 (0.5B parameter) [26], and GR00T N1.5 uses Eagle-2.5 (2.1B parameter) [7]. To isolate the analysis of guide compre-hension from potential errors in fine-grained contact point detection, we conducted experiments on a separate dataset without asset position randomization. This allowed us to fo-cus on whether the models understand the core information types provided by the guides. Further dataset details and training details are provided in the supplementary material. For evaluation, policies are deployed in the simulation and infer actions at 10Hz. The episode is deemed successful if the task’s success criteria (as in Sec. 4.3) are met within a 10-second time limit. Each policy is evaluated with 8 roll-outs per (object, task variation) pair. ## 5.2. Results 

5.2.1. Overall Task Success Rate 

The results of overall tasks are shown in Fig. 5. We observe two clear and central findings across all baseline models. First, the G-VLA policies, trained on the dataset with vi-sual guides, do not exhibit a clear performance improve-ment over the NG-VLA policies, which never observed the guides. For instance, the π0 baseline only marginally from 12.1% (NG-VLA) to 14.1% (G-VLA), and GR00T N1.5 increases from 17.2% to 18.5%. SmolVLA even shows a slight performance decrease (10.1% to 7.4%). These results suggest that the models are not effectively grounding the se-mantic meaning of the in-situ visual guides. In some cases, the guides may even act as visual distractors, particularly for smaller models. Second, the LI-VLA policies achieve substantially higher success rates than both NG-VLA and G-VLA poli-cies. Performance for GR00T N1.5 jumps from 18.5% (G-VLA) to 24.3% (LI-VLA), and for π0 from 14.1% to 18.5%. This demonstrates that the models’ failures are not due to an inability to execute the required physical actions (e.g., squeezing, rotating, pulling). Rather, the bottleneck is purely in perception and understanding. When the semantic meaning of the visual guide is decoded and provided as a language string, the models can successfully leverage this information to solve the guide-conditioned constraints.

5.2.3. Realworld Validation 

Using GR00T, we fine-tune simulation-pretrained weights with LoRA on a small set of real demonstrations, collect-ing 15 trajectories per task variation. We evaluate bottle-std opening tasks (CW/CCW) and a cabinet task (left/right) with 10 trials per setting Fig. 7. Consistent with simulation, LI-VLA outperforms G-VLA on Bottle-Std (65% vs 45%), while G-VLA achieves comparable success to LI-VLA on Cabinet (50% vs 45%). This confirms that the challenge of grounding in-situ guides persists in the real world. 

5.2.4. Summary of Findings and Implications 

Our task-specific analysis reveals that model capabilities are inconsistent and dependent on the information type and model backbone. We find that VLA models can learn to ground simple target disambiguation information from vi-sual guides ( e.g . Cabinet task). However, they largely fail to ground more complex procedural guidance , direc-tional action constraints , or in-part contact specification 

from visual-only cues. Translating this information into lan-guage (LI-VLA) can be effective, but its success is not guaranteed. While procedural information ( e.g . Door task) is broadly beneficial, grounding fine-grained physical in-structions ( e.g . Bottle Squeeze task) is dependent on the VLM’s backbone. These findings show that while in-situ guide can provide significant performance gains, these gains are not robust across models or tasks. This highlights a clear research gap and the need for new approaches specif-ically designed to robustly ground in-situ visual guides and their corresponding physical constraints without relying on external language instructions. 

# 6. Conclusion 

We introduced INSIGHT Bench, a benchmark for robotic manipulation grounded in in-situ text and symbols phys-ically embedded on objects. By explicitly framing in-situ guidance as a core yet previously unexamined problem in manipulation, our benchmark highlights the rich and action-able information these guides provide—information that has remained largely untapped by existing models. By cou-pling these visual guides with explicit, guide-conditioned physical constraints, the benchmark enables systematic evaluation of how agents use object-centric instructions in realistic settings. Our experiments reveal that current VLA models can exploit simple target disambiguation from vi-sual guides but largely fail on directional, procedural, and in-part contact information, exposing a substantial gap in visual grounding for manipulation. This gap demonstrates that, despite the prevalence and utility of in-situ guides in real-world objects, today’s models are not equipped to inter-pret them. These results underscore the need for new models and learning paradigms that directly address in-situ guid-ance, rather than relying solely on externally provided in-structions.
